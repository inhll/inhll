Nobody at Air Canada
owned the corpus.

Jul 1, 2026 5 min read Named case

Air Canada's chatbot didn't invent a refund policy because the model was bad. It invented one because nobody owned the corpus.

That distinction matters more than it sounds, because it changes what you do about it. If the problem is a bad model, you wait for a better one. If the problem is that no one is accountable for what your AI knows, no model upgrade will ever save you.

What actually happened.

A passenger booked a flight shortly after his grandmother died. Before booking, he asked Air Canada's chatbot how the bereavement fare worked. The chatbot told him he could apply for the bereavement discount within 90 days of purchase — even after flying. Air Canada's actual policy says the opposite: no retroactive bereavement refunds.

The chatbot was running on an unverified knowledge base. Nobody had checked whether what it believed about bereavement policy was still true, complete, or correctly structured for retrieval. When the passenger applied for the refund after his trip, Air Canada declined and offered a $200 coupon. He took the airline to the B.C. Civil Resolution Tribunal.

The tribunal ruled it negligent misrepresentation. Air Canada paid $812.02. The chatbot's "separate legal entity" defense — the claim that the airline wasn't responsible for what its own bot said — failed, as these defenses tend to.

The amount is trivial. The accountability question isn't.

Eight hundred dollars is a rounding error for an airline. But the mechanism the ruling established is not: your AI speaks for your company because it runs on your company's data. Whatever you put in — or failed to update — is what comes out under your logo, with full confidence, to real customers.

This is where most coverage of the case stopped, at "AI can be wrong and you're liable." But the more useful question is why the corpus was wrong in the first place, and who was supposed to catch it.

Most companies shipping AI assistants right now have no answer to a simple question: who owns the corpus? Not who built it — who maintains it? Who verifies that what it says about your product, your policies, and your users is still true today, not just the day it was written?

That gap isn't a model problem. It's a governance problem. Specifically, a corpus governance problem.

Hallucination is often a corpus problem wearing a costume.

It's tempting to file the Air Canada incident under "hallucination" and move on — a quirk of the model, something better guardrails will fix. But that framing lets the real failure off the hook. The model retrieved something that didn't match reality. Either the correct policy wasn't in the corpus, wasn't structured so it could be retrieved accurately, or the corpus contained something wrong. Every one of those is a corpus-ownership failure, not a flash of machine imagination.

Guardrails don't close this gap either. Guardrails constrain how the model speaks — tone, safety, format. They can't verify whether what it knows is still true. A guardrail applied to a wrong answer produces a politely wrong answer, delivered with exactly the confidence that got Air Canada into a tribunal.

And "our IT team owns the knowledge base" isn't the same thing. Maintaining the infrastructure that stores documents is a different skill from reviewing those documents for accuracy against current user and policy reality. The second one is research work, and at most companies nobody is assigned to it.

The fix is boring, and it's a process.

If Air Canada had a process for auditing what their AI believed — checking the policy docs in the retrieval layer, verifying they were current, flagging gaps and contradictions — they would have found the bereavement policy error before a tribunal did. That process has a shape: inventory what feeds the AI, date-stamp it, verify it against reality, map where stale beliefs are most likely to cause harm, and put a cadence in place so it stays verified.
