Most GenAI pilots show no return.
The exceptions share one habit.

Jul 15, 2026 5 min read Evidence-led

MIT researchers looked at hundreds of enterprise GenAI deployments and found 95% showed no measurable P&L return. The interesting part isn't the number. It's their diagnosis.

First, the honest caveats.

This stat went viral in 2025 and got beaten up a bit — worth acknowledging that directly. MIT's NANDA initiative built its findings on roughly 150 leader surveys, 52 interviews, and 300 public deployments. "No measurable return" meant no detectable P&L impact — not "the project exploded." Reasonable people have questioned the sample size and the definition of failure.

Fine. Even discounted heavily, the direction matches what practitioners across industries report. The number may be imprecise. The phenomenon is real.

Here's what survived the scrutiny.

MIT's diagnosis was not bad models. It was not poor vendor selection. It was not insufficient budget. They called it a learning gap: the failed pilots were static systems — tools that don't retain context, don't adapt to the organization, don't improve with feedback. The 5% that showed measurable returns were systems wired to keep learning from the business they live in.

Read that again as a founder: the dividing line was not capability. It was adaptivity. Whether the system kept learning.

What "kept learning" actually requires.

A continuous supply of fresh, accurate, organization-specific ground truth. Information about what your customers actually need, believe, and do — collected regularly, structured consistently, and routed into the system that's supposed to represent that knowledge.

Where does ground truth about your customers come from? Not from your AI's own outputs — that's the recursive failure mode Post 6 covers. Not from last year's persona deck. Not from analytics that tell you what users did but can't tell you why, or what's changing underneath the behavior. From structured, recurring contact with actual users.

The learning gap is a research gap wearing a trench coat.

Companies treat user research as a discovery-phase cost and AI as a deployment-phase product. The 5% understand they're the same system: research is how the machine eats. Fresh user knowledge is the ingredient that separates an AI that keeps getting more accurate from one that confidently decays.

The practical implication.

If your GenAI pilot is stalling — if you've deployed something and the returns haven't materialized — the instinct is usually to look at the model, the prompt engineering, the vendor. Those may be real levers. But before pulling them, ask: what is this system learning from? When was the ground truth it draws on last verified against what users actually experience today?

If the answer is "we set it up and haven't revisited it," you're in the 95%. The fix is not a better model. It's a cadence that keeps the model's knowledge base current.
