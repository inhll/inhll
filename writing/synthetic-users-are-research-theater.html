Synthetic users are
research theater.

Jul 8, 2026 5 min read Contrarian

Asking an AI what your users think is asking your existing data what it already says — and paying a subscription for the privilege.

"Synthetic users" are having a moment: simulated customers you can interview at 2am, panels that never churn, insights without the friction of recruiting. The pitch is research without the hard parts.

The problem is that the hard parts were the research.

What the evidence says.

Nielsen Norman Group tested synthetic users and found the answers plausible, generic, and suspiciously agreeable. NN/g's conclusion: they're useful for desk research and hypothesis generation — explicitly not for decisions. That's the most charitable reading from the field's most credible evaluator.

ACM Interactions went further, calling AI-generated research "the illusion of evidence-based decision-making" — research theater. You get a document that looks like research. The document reflects what the model already knows about users like yours, laundered through a simulated interview format. The appearance of evidence without the substance of contact.

My favorite detail from practitioners who've actually run these tools in parallel with real research: ask synthetic users about course completion and they report tidy, rational success stories. Real participants tell you about the job change, the sick kid, the browser tab they abandoned and never reopened. The mess is the signal. Synthetic users don't have lives, so they can't surprise you. And surprise is the only thing research can tell you that you didn't already know.

The deeper structural problem.

There's a more fundamental issue than output quality. In 2024, Shumailov and colleagues published "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data" in Nature. The finding: when models train on output from other models instead of real human data, quality degrades across generations. Rare cases disappear first, then the middle gets mushy and generic.

Synthetic user research is that failure mode, applied voluntarily, to the one input your company can least afford to corrupt: your understanding of your customers. You're feeding AI-generated "user" responses into the knowledge base that trains your next round of AI reasoning about users. Each cycle compresses away the edges — the unexpected behaviors, the surprising contexts, the things real people actually do that no model would predict.

Fresh human data isn't a nice-to-have in that paper. It's stated explicitly as the corrective.

The scope where synthetic users are fine.

To be precise about this: simulated users are genuinely useful for piloting an interview protocol before you take it into the field, or pressure-testing a survey instrument before launch. Use them there. The failure mode isn't using AI to help with research — it's substituting AI-generated responses for actual human contact when decisions are on the line.

If your roadmap, your positioning, or your company AI is learning about users from an AI — you've built a hall of mirrors and called it a moat.
