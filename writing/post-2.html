Proprietary data rots.
Here's the science.

Jun 24, 2026 5 min read Evidence-led

Your data moat has a half-life.

Machine learning researchers have a name for what happens to models in production: concept drift. The relationship between your training data and the reality it describes changes — users shift behavior, markets move, competitors launch — and the model keeps right on predicting. Confidently. Wrongly.

That's the dangerous part. A drifting model doesn't announce failure. It degrades silently while reporting high confidence, right up until a decision built on its output lands badly enough to make the mismatch visible. By that point, you've already shipped the wrong roadmap, or your chatbot has already said something you can't take back.

Apply this to the AI your company just deployed.

Most company AI today is RAG: a language model that answers questions by retrieving relevant content from your internal knowledge base — help docs, personas, research reports, support tickets, internal wikis. The model itself may be state of the art. But its accuracy has a hard ceiling, and that ceiling is the freshness of the documents it retrieves from.

Think about what's actually in that knowledge base. Help documentation from two pricing models ago. User personas built from a research sprint in 2023. Win/loss notes from before your main competitor pivoted. A product positioning doc that nobody has touched since the last reorg.

Your model retrieves all of this faithfully and assembles a confident answer. The answer reflects what was true when those documents were written — which may be very different from what's true now. RAG's accuracy depends entirely on its knowledge base. Stale corpus in, confident nonsense out.

The model isn't broken. It's faithfully retrieving expired truth.

The vendor-side fix doesn't reach the problem.

Every AI vendor will sell you improvements on the model side: better retrieval, larger context windows, smarter agents, fancier embeddings. These are real improvements. None of them address the actual problem, because the problem isn't intelligence — it's ingredients.

Better retrieval delivers the stale document faster. A larger context window means the model can hold more outdated material at once. Smarter agents efficiently act on premises that stopped being true last quarter. The sophistication of the system is not the bottleneck when the bottleneck is the freshness of what that system knows.

The fix is unglamorous.

Scheduled collection of fresh ground truth from real users, flowing into the knowledge base on a cadence. Not a research project with a start date and an end date — a research practice with a weekly rhythm that runs continuously.

The mechanics: a lightweight intake form so any team can submit a question, weekly triage to prioritize and sequence, moderated sessions throughout the week, and Flash Findings — structured, dated outputs that answer the question asked. Crucially, outputs that are formatted consistently enough that your retrieval system can index and surface them alongside everything else it knows.

The cadence is what the technology requires. There is no correct model-side fix for a knowledge base whose contents stopped being accurate. The corrective is collection — regular, structured contact with the users whose reality the system is supposed to reflect.

How fast does it actually decay? That depends on your market's velocity. In fast-moving categories, core user assumptions can break inside a quarter. There's no universal constant, which is exactly why you measure it with a cadence rather than guessing — and why the cadence can't be a one-time project.
