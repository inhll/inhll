Air Canada's chatbot made a promise.
A tribunal made them keep it.

Jul 1, 2026 5 min read Provocative

Air Canada argued in a tribunal that its own chatbot was "a separate legal entity responsible for its own actions."

That defense went exactly as well as you'd expect.

The backstory.

A passenger booked a flight shortly after his grandmother died. Before booking, he consulted Air Canada's chatbot to understand the airline's bereavement fare policy. The chatbot told him he could apply for the bereavement discount within 90 days of purchase — even after flying.

Air Canada's actual policy says no retroactive bereavement refunds. The chatbot had, in plain terms, made it up.

When the passenger applied for the refund after his trip, Air Canada declined and offered a $200 coupon. He took the airline to the B.C. Civil Resolution Tribunal. Air Canada's legal defense included the argument that the chatbot operated independently and the airline wasn't responsible for what it said. The tribunal called this argument "remarkable."

The ruling found negligent misrepresentation. Air Canada paid $812.02.

The precedent is not the payout.

Eight hundred dollars is a rounding error for an airline. The ruling is not. The tribunal held that a chatbot "is still just a part of Air Canada's website" and the company is responsible for everything it says. The "separate entity" defense — the idea that a company can deploy an AI and disclaim its outputs — was rejected clearly and on the record.

Your AI speaks for your company. Legally.

One clarification worth making honestly: this was a hallucination case, not primarily a stale-data case. The chatbot didn't retrieve an old policy document — it fabricated a policy that never existed. But the ruling's logic applies equally to both failure modes. Whether your AI is confabulating or faithfully surfacing outdated information, the company behind it is responsible for what gets said.

The verification gap.

Here's the question the Air Canada case puts to every founder shipping a customer-facing AI this year: who, at your company, verifies that what your AI believes matches what's currently true — about your policies, your product, and your users?

At most companies the honest answer is nobody. There's no owner, no process, no cadence. The knowledge base was accurate the day it was written, and it has been quietly diverging from reality ever since. Customer-facing queries are being answered by a system whose beliefs have never been formally audited against what's currently true.

The chatbot doesn't know it's out of date. It will answer anyway — with total confidence, under your logo, with your company's legal exposure attached.

There is a discipline for keeping a company's stated beliefs synced to reality. It's called research operations: the systematic, scheduled practice of collecting fresh user and market ground truth and routing it back into the systems that claim to know what customers need. It's not glamorous. It's a lot cheaper than discovering your AI's imagination in a tribunal filing.
