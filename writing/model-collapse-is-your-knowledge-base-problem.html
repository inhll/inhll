Model collapse isn't coming for the internet.
It's coming for your knowledge base.

Jul 22, 2026 5 min read Contrarian

The Nature paper everyone read as an OpenAI problem is actually a your-company problem.

In 2024, Shumailov and colleagues published "AI models collapse when trained on recursively generated data" in Nature. The finding: when models train on output from other models instead of real human data, quality degrades across generations. Rare cases disappear first — the tails of the distribution — then the middle gets mushy and generic. They demonstrated the effect across multiple model families.

Everyone discussed it as an internet-scale doom scenario: the web fills with AI-generated text, future models are trained on it, the quality of the training data spirals downward, civilization's collective knowledge base spoils. Researchers still debate how severe that gets at internet scale, and fairly — the worst-case assumes nobody curates anything, which isn't quite true.

But while we argued about the internet version, most companies quietly built the small version inside their own walls.

Look at your own stack.

AI summaries of AI-drafted tickets, pasted into project documentation. Meeting notes generated by a transcription bot, summarized by a second bot, filed in the knowledge base your RAG assistant retrieves from. Personas "refreshed" by asking a model to extrapolate from last year's personas without running new interviews. Internal wiki articles written by an AI assistant drawing on previous internal wiki articles.

Every loop like this replaces a human observation with a model's compression of one. Information about what users actually think and do is replaced with a model's prediction of what information about users would look like. The mechanism is identical to the one in the paper — it's just running at team scale instead of internet scale.

The tails vanish first.

In the paper, the tails of the distribution are the first casualty — unusual cases, minority behaviors, edge instances that don't fit the central tendency. In your company's knowledge base, the tails are your most important research findings: the weird workflow that reveals a latent need, the angry churned user whose complaint flags a systemic problem, the participant doing something with your product that nobody designed for. These are exactly the things research exists to catch. They are exactly what gets compressed away when AI summarizes AI summarizes human observations.

The authors are explicit about the corrective: continued access to fresh, real, human-generated data. That's not a metaphor. The paper identifies it as the mechanism that prevents collapse.

What prevents it, operationally.

Inside a company, "fresh human-generated data about users" has an unglamorous name: a research cadence. Sessions on the calendar, every week. Real users talking about real experiences. Outputs dated and structured so the retrieval system knows how current they are. The humans in the loop staying genuinely in the loop — not reviewing AI summaries of AI summaries, but occasionally reading a raw transcript and being surprised.

The critics of the collapse paper are right that curated training data slows the effect. Curation of your company's user knowledge is research. If you accept the critique, you've conceded the thesis.
